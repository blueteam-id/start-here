## What this track covers

The web is the part of the internet most people use, and the part attackers aim at most. Five lessons:

1. **From URL to page** (this lesson): the whole trip, and how to watch it.
2. **HTTP up close**: requests, responses, status codes, and server logs.
3. **HTML, browsers, and search engines**: what a page is made of, and how pages get found.
4. **Cookies and sessions**: how a website remembers you, and how that gets stolen.
5. **Wrap-up**: you trace one real page load from end to end.

## Reading a URL

A **URL** is the full address of something on the web. Take this one apart:

```
https://shop.example.com:443/products/shoes?color=blue#reviews
```

- `https` is the **scheme**: the protocol to use. `https` means HTTP over an encrypted connection.
- `shop.example.com` is the **host**. The part that matters most is the end: `example.com` is the **domain**, and `shop` is a **subdomain** of it.
- `443` is the **port** (Networking Lesson 3). It is usually left out, because 443 is the default for `https` and 80 for `http`.
- `/products/shoes` is the **path**: which page or file on that server.
- `?color=blue` is the **query string**: extra values sent to the server.
- `#reviews` is the **fragment**: a spot inside the page. It is never sent to the server.

<Callout type="warning" title="Read hosts from right to left">

Phishing links abuse how people read. In `https://login.example.com.attacker.net/`, the real domain is `attacker.net`; everything before it is just a subdomain the attacker chose. Always find the domain at the right end of the host, just before the first `/`.

</Callout>

## The trip, step by step

When you press Enter on a URL, roughly this happens:

1. **DNS**: the browser looks up the host's IP address (Networking Lesson 4).
2. **TCP**: it opens a connection to that address on port 443, with the three-way handshake.
3. **TLS**: it sets up encryption and checks the site's **certificate** (Security Concepts Lesson 3).
4. **HTTP request**: it asks for the path, for example `GET /products/shoes`.
5. **HTTP response**: the server answers with a status code and the page's HTML (Lesson 2).
6. **More requests**: the HTML mentions images, styles, scripts, and fonts, often from other domains. The browser fetches each one.
7. **Rendering**: the browser builds the page you see, and runs its scripts.

A single page often needs dozens or hundreds of requests. Many go to other companies: content delivery networks, analytics, ads, fonts. Every one of those is a domain your browser trusted on that site's behalf.

## Hands-on: watch a page load

Every browser has **developer tools** (DevTools) built in. Open them with `F12`, or `Ctrl+Shift+I` on Windows and Linux, or `Cmd+Option+I` on macOS. Then:

1. Click the **Network** tab.
2. Visit `https://example.com`.
3. Look at the list. Each row is one request. Note the **Status**, **Type**, and **Domain** (or Name) columns.

`example.com` is tiny: one or two requests. Now keep DevTools open and visit a big news or shopping site you use.

Write down:

- How many requests the page made (the count is at the bottom of the Network tab).
- How many different domains appear. Some tools let you add a **Domain** column by right-clicking the column headers.
- One domain you do not recognize. Look up what it is.

<Callout type="tip">

If the list is empty, DevTools was opened after the page loaded. Keep it open and reload the page.

</Callout>

### What to notice

- A URL has a scheme, host, port, path, query, and fragment. The domain at the right end of the host is what matters for trust.
- One page load is DNS, TCP, TLS, then many HTTP requests, often to many other domains.
- DevTools' Network tab shows every request your browser makes. Defenders and developers both live in it.
