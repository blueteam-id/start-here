## Data leakage

LLM features can leak information in several ways. OWASP groups them under **Sensitive Information Disclosure** (LLM02) and **System Prompt Leakage** (LLM07):

- **Through the prompt.** Anything users paste in leaves their control (AI Fundamentals Lesson 5). Secrets in prompts can end up in logs or, depending on the service, in future training data.
- **Through retrieval.** A RAG system that ignores permissions hands people documents they should not see (Lesson 2).
- **Through the system prompt.** Developers sometimes put secrets, internal rules, or even passwords in the system prompt. Prompt injection (Lesson 4) can often make the model reveal it. A system prompt is not a safe place for secrets.
- **Through training data.** Models can sometimes repeat pieces of their training text word for word, so sensitive data used in training can come back out.

## Agents: LLMs that take actions

An **AI agent** is an LLM connected to **tools**: it can search the web, read files, query databases, send emails, run code, or call other services. The model reads a task, decides which tool to use, reads the result, and continues, often over many steps.

Agents are useful, and they change the risk completely. A chatbot that is tricked can only **say** something wrong. An agent that is tricked can **do** something wrong: forward confidential files, delete records, or send emails as you. Combine that with indirect prompt injection (Lesson 4), and any email, web page, or document the agent reads becomes a possible way to give it orders.

OWASP calls giving an LLM more power than it needs **Excessive Agency** (LLM06). The defenses will sound familiar from every earlier track:

- **Least privilege**: give the agent only the tools and permissions its job needs. A summarizer does not need "send email".
- **Human approval** for anything sensitive or irreversible: sending, deleting, paying, changing access.
- **Separate identities**: the agent acts with its own limited account, so its actions are logged and can be cut off.
- **Logging and monitoring** (accounting): record what the agent did and why.

## What you now know

1. **Tokens.** LLMs read tokens; the context window limits what they can see; other languages, code, and random strings cost more tokens.
2. **Embeddings.** Meaning becomes vectors; semantic search and RAG find text by meaning, and must respect permissions.
3. **Next-token prediction.** Transformers use attention to predict one token at a time; models produce likely text, not true text, so they hallucinate.
4. **Prompt injection and jailbreaks.** Instructions and data share one channel; indirect injection hides in content; defenses are layers, not one fix.

## Hands-on: threat-model an AI help-desk agent

Arunika Labs (the company from SQL Basics) plans to launch **HelpBot**, an AI agent for its IT help desk. The plan:

- Employees chat with HelpBot to get help with IT problems.
- HelpBot can **read** all help-desk tickets, including attachments.
- HelpBot can **search** the internal wiki, which includes some HR and finance pages.
- HelpBot can **send emails** from `helpdesk@arunika.example` to anyone.
- HelpBot can **reset any employee's password** and email them a temporary one.

You are asked for a quick security review. On paper, write:

1. **Assets**: three things worth protecting here (Security Concepts Lesson 1).
2. **Entry points for injection**: at least three places where an attacker's text could reach HelpBot.
3. **The worst realistic outcome** if HelpBot were successfully injected. Be specific.
4. **Five changes** you would require before launch, using this track's defenses.

<details>
<summary>What a good review covers</summary>

1. Assets: employee accounts and passwords; confidential HR and finance information in the wiki; the help-desk email address and its trust (people believe mail from it); ticket contents, which can include personal data.
2. Entry points: the chat itself (direct injection); the text of any ticket, since anyone can file one; ticket attachments; wiki pages anyone can edit; incoming emails if they become tickets.
3. Worst case: an attacker files a ticket containing hidden instructions. When HelpBot reads it, it resets a finance manager's password, emails the temporary password to an outside address, and sends convincing phishing to all staff from the real help-desk address. Or it pastes HR salary pages into its answers.
4. Changes: remove free email sending, or allow only replies to the requester's own company address; require a human to approve every password reset, and send temporary passwords only to the account owner through a separate verified channel; limit wiki search to pages the asking employee may already read; treat ticket and attachment text as untrusted data, and never act on instructions found inside it; give HelpBot its own limited account and log every tool call; keep secrets out of the system prompt.

</details>

## Where to go from here

AI security is moving fast, and much of it is still being worked out. The foundations from this program will not go out of date: least privilege, untrusted input, human approval for sensitive actions, logs, and permission before testing.
