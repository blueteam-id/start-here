## Why this lesson exists

So far, everything in this track has been about one computer: its own files, its own permissions, its own local accounts. That is a fair picture of a home computer. It is not how a company works.

A company with a hundred employees does not want to manage a hundred computers one by one, each with its own list of users and passwords. It wants one place to manage everyone and everything at once. That place is **Active Directory**, usually just called **AD**.

You will hear "Active Directory" constantly in security jobs, even if you never touch a Windows desktop, because almost every mid-size or large company runs on it. It is hard to picture if you have never used a company computer, so this lesson uses one simple comparison.

## The badge-office analogy

Picture a company with five office buildings and no Active Directory. Each building has its own door, its own lock, and its own list of who may enter. If you work in two buildings, you need two keys. If you leave the company, someone must remove your name from five lists, one building at a time, and hope they do not forget one.

Now picture the same company with Active Directory. There is one central **badge office**. It gives you one badge, and that badge opens every building, because every building trusts the same badge office. If you leave, the badge office disables your badge once, and every door stops opening for you immediately.

In that picture:

- The **badge office** is a **domain controller**: a special Windows server that holds the master list of every user, every computer, and every rule.
- All the **buildings that trust the same badge office** together form a **domain**. When your work laptop "joins the domain", it agrees to trust that badge office instead of keeping its own private list of users.
- **Active Directory** is the whole system: the database of users, computers, and rules, plus the service that answers "is this badge valid, and what may it open?"

## A few words worth knowing

These Active Directory terms come up often enough to learn now:

- **Domain controller (DC).** The server running the badge office. Companies usually have more than one, so there is a backup if one fails.
- **Domain.** All the users and computers that trust one badge office.
- **Organizational Unit (OU).** A folder-like group inside Active Directory, used to organize users and computers. For example, one OU for Sales and another for IT, so different rules can apply to each.
- **Group Policy.** A way for the badge office to push settings to every computer in the domain at once, such as "lock the screen after five minutes" or "block USB drives". An administrator sets the rule once, centrally.
- **Security group.** Instead of giving one person access to one thing at a time, you put people in a named group (for example, "Finance Team") and give the group access. Adding or removing someone from the group changes what they can reach everywhere, at once.

## Why this matters so much for defenders

Here is what makes Active Directory a security topic and not just an IT convenience. The same badge office that makes life easy for administrators is also the most valuable target for an attacker.

- One stolen ordinary employee account is bad, but it usually opens only a few doors.
- One stolen **Domain Admin** account, the highest level of access in Active Directory, can open every door: every computer, every file share, every mailbox.

This is why most ransomware and other serious intrusions are not really about the first computer an attacker reaches. That computer is just a foothold. The real goal is to climb from "I have one ordinary laptop" to "I have a Domain Admin badge", because from there almost everything is reachable. You will hear two terms for that climb:

- **Lateral movement**: spreading from one computer to others.
- **Privilege escalation**: going from low access to much higher access.

You do not need to know how to do either. This track stays at foundation depth. Recognizing the terms, and understanding why Active Directory makes the prize so big, is the foundation a later, deeper course builds on.

## Checking your own computer

Most personal computers, and the evaluation VM you may be using, are **not** in a domain. Windows calls them part of a **workgroup**, its word for "standalone, not part of a company network". Check yours:

```powershell
Get-CimInstance Win32_ComputerSystem | Select-Object Domain, PartOfDomain
```

If `PartOfDomain` shows `False`, then `Domain` shows `WORKGROUP`. That is the generic name for a computer that trusts no one but itself, exactly what you expect on a personal machine or a standalone VM. On a company laptop that has joined a domain, `PartOfDomain` shows `True`, and `Domain` shows the company's domain name.

`systeminfo` in Lesson 1 also printed a `Domain:` line. That was the same fact inside a long report. This command asks for just that one piece of information.

### What to notice

- Active Directory solves one problem: managing users, computers, and rules for a whole company from one place.
- A Domain Admin account is the most valuable target in a company's Windows network, because it is the one badge that opens every door.
- "Lateral movement" and "privilege escalation" describe the climb from one foothold to that powerful account. That climb is the real goal behind most serious intrusions.
