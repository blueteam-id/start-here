## Four outcomes for every alert

Whenever a detector decides "malicious" or "benign", there are four possible outcomes:

| | Really malicious | Really benign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Detector says malicious** | **True positive (TP)**: caught it | **False positive (FP)**: false alarm |
| **Detector says benign** | **False negative (FN)**: missed it | **True negative (TN)**: correctly ignored |

This table is called a **confusion matrix**. Two numbers summarize it:

- **Precision** = TP ÷ (TP + FP). Of all the alerts, how many were real? Low precision means analysts waste time on false alarms.
- **Recall** = TP ÷ (TP + FN). Of all the real attacks, how many were caught? Low recall means attacks slip through.

Improving one often hurts the other. Make the detector more sensitive and recall goes up, but so do false alarms.

## The base rate problem

Attacks are **rare** compared with normal activity. That changes everything.

Imagine 10,000 normal logins and 10 malicious ones in a day. A detector catches 9 of the 10 attacks (recall 90%) and wrongly flags just 1% of normal logins. That sounds excellent. Now count:

- True positives: **9**.
- False positives: 1% of 10,000 = **100**.
- Precision: 9 ÷ (9 + 100) = **about 8%**.

More than 9 out of 10 alerts are false alarms, from a detector that looked great on paper. This is the **base rate fallacy**, and it is the main reason for **alert fatigue**: analysts who see mostly false alarms start to ignore alerts, including the real ones.

It also explains why "99% accuracy" means little. A detector that says "benign" to **everything** would be 99.9% accurate on that day, and catch nothing.

## Other ways models fail

- **Bias**: a model repeats the patterns in its training data, including unfair or unrepresentative ones. A detector trained mostly on one company's traffic may misjudge another's.
- **Data poisoning**: an attacker who can influence the training data can teach the model wrong lessons, for example to treat their malware as clean.
- **Adversarial examples**: small, deliberate changes to an input that flip the model's answer, such as padding a malicious file with harmless-looking content until a classifier calls it clean.
- **Drift** (Lesson 2): the world changes and the model slowly stops matching it.

## Hands-on: measure a detector

An AI-based alerting tool ran for one week. Afterwards, analysts checked everything and filled in this confusion matrix:

| | Really malicious | Really benign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tool said malicious** | 40 | 160 |
| **Tool said benign** | 10 | 9,790 |

On paper, work out:

1. How many alerts did the tool raise in total?
2. Its **precision**.
3. Its **recall**.
4. If your team can only investigate 50 alerts a week, roughly how many real attacks would you expect to find, if you picked alerts at random?

<details>
<summary>Check your answers</summary>

1. Alerts = TP + FP = 40 + 160 = **200**.
2. Precision = 40 ÷ 200 = **20%**. Four out of five alerts are false alarms.
3. Recall = 40 ÷ (40 + 10) = **80%**. One in five real attacks was missed.
4. At 20% precision, 50 random alerts contain about **10** real attacks, out of 40 that were caught. Better ranking of alerts, or better precision, matters as much as catching more.

</details>

<Callout type="tip" title="Questions to ask about any detector">

What is its precision and recall **on data like ours**? How many alerts per day would it raise for us? How was it tested, and how is it kept up to date? A vendor who only quotes "accuracy" has not answered the question.

</Callout>

### What to notice

- Every detector has true and false positives and negatives. Precision is "alerts that were real"; recall is "attacks that were caught".
- Because attacks are rare, even a small false-alarm rate can bury the real alerts.
- "Accuracy" is misleading on rare events. Ask for precision and recall on your kind of data.
- Models can also be biased, poisoned, fooled with crafted inputs, or left behind by drift.
